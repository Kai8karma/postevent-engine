# From Hype to High-Impact: How to Start and Scale AI in HR

*From Hype to High-Impact: How to Start and Scale AI in HR — Darwinbox, August 13, 2026 — Q Hamirani, Chief People Officer, HighLevel and Sudi Bjornstad Korba, Senior Vice President of Sales, North America, Darwinbox*

## Why We Chose Darwinbox: Scaling HRIS for Hypergrowth

When Q Hamirani joined HighLevel 10 months ago, the company had approximately 1,000 employees. Today, it has grown to nearly 1,800 employees across 10 countries, with an average monthly hiring rate of about 100 people. This rapid expansion exposed the limitations of fragmented HR systems operating in silos across different regions. As Q explained, “we did not have NHRIs, a true global NHRIs. We had different systems in different countries that ran NHRIs, ran payroll.” The lack of a unified platform meant inconsistent processes, limited insights, and a poor employee experience.

The decision to adopt Darwinbox centered on three non-negotiables: global scalability, local compliance, and usability for all stakeholders. Q emphasized the need for a system that could handle U.S. state-specific time tracking laws while also meeting India’s statutory requirements. Beyond compliance, Darwinbox’s embedded analytics capabilities eliminated the need to build a dedicated people analytics team from scratch. “Because the analytics are so rich and embedded… I don’t need to build a people analytics team that I’ve had to build in previous organizations,” Q noted. The platform’s storyboards allow HR leaders to surface insights directly from people data without heavy upfront investment in analytics headcount.

## AI in Compensation: From Manual Analysis to Real-Time Insights

One of the most tangible applications of AI at HighLevel has been in compensation management. Initially, Q planned to build a compensation team of 2 to 3 people to manage market data analysis and internal leveling alignment. Instead, the team trained its own AI model using subscribed Radford data to deliver near real-time compensation recommendations based on job descriptions. As Q stated, “we've been able to train our own models to give us almost real-time compensation based on a job description and alignment with our internal leveling framework.”

This shift has dramatically accelerated a process that once took days. The AI model can scan 8,000 to 9,000 jobs in Radford within minutes, providing comparative market data that informs pay decisions. However, Q stressed that this is not a “set and forget” solution. “It's not a one and done thing,” he cautioned, noting that ongoing fine-tuning is essential. HR professionals must validate outputs against business context and their own expertise to correct mismatches — such as when the model maps a job to an incorrect code — ensuring AI augments rather than replaces human judgment.

## Guardrails for Responsible AI: Experimentation, Privacy, and Human Oversight

Adopting AI in HR requires deliberate guardrails, especially when handling sensitive employee data. Q described a phased approach: starting with controlled experimentation in sandbox environments before scaling. “You have to start from a place of experimenting with caution,” he said, recalling initial tests with enterprise Chat GPT to understand how AI processed compensation queries. Only after validating the model internally did the team begin feeding it live HR data.

Key safeguards include limiting access to the compensation model to just two or three people on his team of 50, partnering with IT and engineering teams to vet data security protocols, and using tools like MCP (Model Context Protocol) to ensure data flows with built-in role-based security and compliance. Q also emphasized the importance of human-in-the-loop checkpoints, particularly when AI begins making decisions rather than just automating tasks. “You have to figure out where you want to have kind of human in the loop checkpoints,” he advised, noting that workflows should include audits, anomaly detection, and validation with business partners to prevent hallucination or misuse.

## AI as a Capacity Unlock, Not a Job Replacement

Addressing fears about AI displacing HR roles, Q reframed the narrative: AI doesn’t eliminate jobs — it unlocks capacity. “It's taken away a small subset of jobs and unlocked capacity from those individuals to do things that they were not able to do before,” he explained. This perspective draws parallels to past technological shifts, like the adoption of Excel, which automated manual calculations and freed professionals to focus on higher-value analysis.

At HighLevel, this has meant redeploying time saved through AI-driven compensation analytics and automated expense reporting toward more strategic, human-centric work. Q highlighted that while AI can handle data processing and routine tasks efficiently, it cannot replicate the nuanced understanding required in performance coaching, change management, or building authentic employee connections. “The true human connection… cannot be replaced in work or even in life truly with AI,” he affirmed. Instead, AI serves as a teammate that amplifies human skills — drafting communications, surfacing insights, or summarizing information — which HR professionals then enrich with empathy, context, and relational intelligence.

## Where AI Falls Short: The Irreplaceable Human Elements

Despite AI’s growing capabilities, Q was clear about its limits. In recruiting, for example, AI can screen resumes and schedule interviews, but it cannot replicate the relational depth of a conversation where a candidate shares their motivations or concerns. Similarly, in performance management, while AI can track metrics and identify trends, it cannot unpack the underlying behaviors driving those numbers or guide empathetic coaching conversations.

In change management, AI can generate communications or summarize policy updates at scale, but it cannot replace the trust-building dialogue needed to help employees navigate transitions. Q’s advice is clear: use AI to handle the 75–80% of tasks that are rule-based and repetitive, then apply human judgment to the remaining 20–25% where context, empathy, and relational insight are essential. This partnership model allows HR teams to do more with less — not by cutting headcount, but by elevating the quality and impact of their work.
